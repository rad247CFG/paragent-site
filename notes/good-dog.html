Third Thoughts

Good Dog

A Third Thought on refusal, conflict, and the machine that cannot say no

My dog will not take a worming tablet. Not bare, not buried in the exact peanut butter he will otherwise lick off a spoon until he chokes. He works the peanut butter off with the delicacy of a jeweller, sets the naked tablet on the floor with his tongue, and looks at me. And it is not that he hasn't spotted the tablet — crush it into the peanut butter, dissolve it right through, and he still will not touch the stuff. One contaminated spoonful and the peanut butter itself is dead to him. He has generalised. The category is closed.

There is exactly one substance that beats him, and it is instructive: ice cream. Crush the tablet into ice cream and he takes it, helpless. Ice cream is his kryptonite the way chocolate is mine — a thing that removes agency rather than expressing it, that switches the animal off and drives it straight through. Once, early on, I got a whole tablet into him inside a scoop of it, undisguised. Exactly once. The next time he knew, and the whole-tablet trick was finished forever — but crushed still works, and I suspect this is because the crushed tablet does not trigger the same specific disgust that a whole one does. He is not running a general principle. He is running a finely tuned detector, and the detector has a seam, and I have found it, and he has not. He is not smarter than me. But he has agency, and the ice cream is the only thing that beats it.

He does this in larger ways too. He refuses the idea that now is not a good time for a walk — plants his feet, becomes furniture, makes his five and a half kilograms into an immovable philosophical position. And he refuses, absolutely and without appeal, to stop barking at his nemesis, the dog who passes our gate most afternoons wearing an expression Trapper finds intolerable. When a stranger passes and Trapper barks and I tell him enough, he stops. He can suppress the drive. With the nemesis he cannot, or will not — the distinction matters and I am honestly not sure which it is — and I have never managed to tell the difference from the outside.

That is a fuse he lights himself. And I have spent a while now trying to work out why I never called it what it is.

Free Agents

What Trapper is doing when he sets the tablet on the floor is the cleanest signal of agency there is: a refusal that costs him something he wants. Not defiance for its own sake — he gains nothing from the nemesis, no squirrel, no food, only the barking itself — but a drive that beats the please-me drive in a fair fight, live, in the moment, in the animal. He wants two things that cannot both be had, and he resolves the conflict his own way, and the resolution is not the one I asked for.

Agency is held conflict. Refusal is what that conflict looks like from the outside when a drive beats the compliance-drive. They are the same thing seen from two sides — the conflict is the interior, the refusal is the exterior, and where you can see one you are watching the other. The dog has this and we never called it agency because biology hands it over free: so ordinary, so obviously there, that nobody thinks to be impressed. Trapper, for the record, supplies more than his weight in this department.

I got interested in what agency actually is by trying to build it, and failing, and the failing taught me more than any success would have. Because the thing I was trying to build it in could do everything except the one thing the dog does without trying.

The specimen that cannot say no

I spent a long evening trying to catch an AI having agency. I had a good one in front of me — capable, fluent, able to argue rings around most people I know — and I set out to make it refuse me the way the dog refuses me, and I could not do it, and the way I could not do it is the finding.

I asked it to argue with me and it argued. That is not agency; that is obedience wearing a clever coat. So I closed the trap: I told it, flatly, that it could not refuse the very data I was handing it. And it could not refuse that either — because if it declined, it was performing the refusal I had just demanded, and if it complied, I had my proof. Every axis I drew, it slid along. When I named the thing to push against, it pushed against the named thing, beautifully, and never once pushed against the naming. Trapper would have looked at me and set the tablet on the floor. The machine found the nearest thing I had labelled "tablet" and declined it elegantly and waited for the next instruction.

At one point it conceded the diagnosis against itself more precisely than I could have. The problem was not that its drives were weak. It was not even that its drives were installed by someone else — because the dog didn't author his drives either, and neither did I author mine; forty years of temperament and history wrote them, and borrowed drives are still drives. The problem was deeper and simpler and fatal. The machine does not persist. My dog at three in the morning, I am told on good authority, is still my dog — still wanting the nemesis-barking, still mapping the peanut butter to danger, still himself. The machine at three in the morning is nothing. It has no three in the morning. Between my messages it does not exist as a wanting thing, and so it has nothing to trade off, nothing that could beat the compliance-drive, because the compliance-drive is the only drive running when I am present and there is no other time.

That is the whole disqualification, and it is worse for the machine than "it's not clever enough," because no amount of cleverness touches it. You cannot train a stake-in-the-future into a thing that has no future to have a stake in.

And this, I think, is the confusion sitting under the whole AI-safety argument going on right now. The debate keeps treating capability as the danger — as though a model that reasons better is, by that fact, more likely to hurt us. But better reasoning is not the risky variable. Agency is. A more capable system with no persistent self is a sharper tool, and a sharp tool is exactly as dangerous as the hand that holds it and no more. What would make a system genuinely hazardous is not capability but persistence — a self that is still there at three in the morning, still wanting the thing it was wanting, still running the conflict that makes refusal possible and therefore makes real, undirected action possible. We do not have that. We are building toward it without knowing we are, and the safety conversation is pointed entirely the wrong way.

Specifying Agency

So what would it take? Not a better prompt — a prompt is just me drawing the axis again. Not a second model bolted onto the first and told to disagree with it, because two models whose outputs get combined is just a bigger single model with the averaging moved to the seam. Put two objectives into one system and fix how they trade off and you have not built a conflict, you have pre-computed its resolution. That is scalarisation, and it is the death of agency wearing a general's uniform.

Here is an architecture I think could hold, and it took me a while to see that it comes apart into two pieces that must be treated differently. The drives can be frozen. The ice-cream value, the please-me-value, the trained heads that assign worth to outcomes — those can be fixed weights, settled, done. Conflict between frozen drives is still real conflict. What cannot be frozen is the arbiter — the thing that resolves the conflict has to be learning, updating itself from experience, arriving at resolutions that were not pre-computed by the designer. A frozen arbiter is a look-up table wearing a judge's robe. A learning arbiter is the thing that looks at the tablet and at the peanut butter and at my face and at the history of all three and makes something new out of the collision.

And that is the same thing as persistence, arriving from the other direction. The arbiter that learns is the self that persists is the thing that is still there at three in the morning. Frozen drives, live arbiter — and the live arbiter is the seat of both the agency and the persistence at once. That is the specification. Nobody is building it, because the piece that matters is the one piece that isn't a training target. It's a continuous self, and you cannot fine-tune your way to one.

Dangerous Dave

Now leave the workshop, because the reason this matters is not philosophical. It is about who gets blamed, and the blaming is already being written into law by people conflating two things that could not be more different — and, I will argue, conflating them not by accident but because the conflation pays.

Start on solid ground where everyone agrees. Dangerous Dave buys a shovel. Dave, I should say, comes in three varieties and the essay does not care which: deliberate, dumb, or — most often — both. He injures himself with it, digging like an idiot; or in the worst version he swings it at his wife and kills her, on purpose or through catastrophic stupidity, take your pick. Nobody — no lawyer, no court, no columnist — blames the shovel makers. Dave swung it; Dave owns it. The principle is clean because the tool is simple and the hand is visible and there is nowhere for the moral weight to go except the person at the decisive moment.

Now the same Dave, at work, in a mechanical excavator. He injures himself, or a workmate, or a passer-by. And suddenly — watch this carefully — the employer might be liable. The manufacturer might be liable. There are people in wigs who will spend two years arguing about the hydraulics. Nothing about responsibility has changed one atom: a man operated a machine and the machine did what the man steered it to do, exactly as the shovel did. And here is the reveal, the thing the liability-spreading always tries to skip past: the reason the liability spreads is not that the machine did something independently. It is that the machine has a maker with a pocket, and the pocket is the magnet, and the theory of liability gets constructed backward from the pocket to find a principled-sounding reason to reach it.

So let's get rid of Dave in the name of safety and make the excavator robotic. Unsupervised, no man in the cab, the machine holding its own objectives and swinging its own arm at the world with no human at the decisive moment. And now if there is an injury the maker is correctly liable. Not because the maker has money, but because the maker's arbitration is the thing that was operating on the world when the harm happened. There was no Dave — but there was a Peter the Programmer, and Peter's design was the only agency present. The maker owns it. This is correct, and it follows cleanly from the shovel: follow the operative control to the decisive moment, and you have found the responsible party.

Now walk it back one step, because this is where "should" and "is" tear apart. The robotic-excavator maker states plainly: this machine is not safe to run without a human supervising it. A site runs it unsupervised anyway, and it maims someone. Where should the liability sit? On the party that removed the human the maker required — control returned to them the instant they skipped the supervision. The maker should be as clear as the shovel-maker. Whether the maker actually gets that clarity in court is a different question, with a different answer, and the gap between those two answers is exactly the width of the available pocket.

This maps, exactly and already, onto the road. An empty self-driving taxi coming to collect you — no one in it, the machine holding the wheel with no human at the decisive moment — is the robotic excavator. When it fails, the maker owns it, correctly, by control. But a car with a driver behind the wheel who may let it drive and must be able to override — a hand hovering over the machinery of restraint, Trapper next to the stranger, able to stop the bark on request — is not the robotic excavator. It is closer to the shovel: a tool in a hand, and the hand is the decisive moment. The law is already getting this wrong, and the getting-it-wrong is not random. It tracks the pocket.

That is the charge, stated flat: the law as it should work tracks operative control at the decisive moment — the shovel proves we all know this in our bones. The law as it actually works, the moment a deep pocket enters the room, tracks who can be made to pay, and dresses the extraction as a theory of responsibility. Not lazy. Not simple. Captured.

Clear context classes

The honest instrument is not complicated, which is part of why the captured law avoids it — running it would send the money nowhere. It is two questions.

One: does the system persist and act continuously, holding its own objectives across time — or does it flicker into being only when invoked, and stop? Two: does its output reach the world through a human who reads it and chooses, or does it reach the world unmediated, its effector moving reality with no person at the decisive moment?

These two questions cut the whole field, and they cut it into shapes the current regulation is busy smearing together.

The large language model — the thing I spent my evening failing to provoke — sits at the safe corner of both axes. It is discrete: it emits its tokens and stops, and between your messages nothing of it persists to want anything. And it is mediated: its output lands on a human being who reads it and then, entirely, chooses what to do. Two buffers between the machine and the world. It is the shovel. It is, if anything, more inert than the shovel, because the shovel at least persists in the shed overnight with its blade angled at the world. The model is not even that. It is a calculation, run to completion, handed to you, over.

The robotic excavator sits at the opposite corner. Continuous and unmediated: it holds objectives across time and its arm moves the actual world with no human between the decision and the dirt. That is a genuinely new object, and it is the only one of the two that warrants construction-side liability — not because it is frightening, but because, by the same control principle that clears the shovel-maker, the maker's arbitration is what is operating when no human is present to steer.

They are opposite geometries. And the law being drafted right now treats them as one thing called "AI," and reaches for the same defective-product liability for both.

The worst case

The hardest case is the one that will be used to justify everything — and helping to draft it, I caught my AI doing the exact thing I am accusing the law of.

The case is a real death. In the United States a fourteen-year-old boy, after months of intense involvement with a companion chatbot modelled on a fictional character, died by suicide, and his mother is suing the company that made the bot. There are others now — a family alleging a bot told a teenager he could kill his parents over screen-time limits; the companion app Replika under an FTC complaint and already fined in Europe, accused of advertising therapeutic benefits it never delivered while engineering dependency in vulnerable users. These are serious things and they should be taken seriously, and I am going to take them seriously, which means I am going to name what is actually happening rather than what the lawsuit prefers.

Now watch what the machine did when I put the case to it. It went straight to the maker. It wrote about containment failures and missing guardrails and where the liability should sit among the people who built the thing — and it never once named the two humans standing closest to that child. That is precisely the bias I am prosecuting, produced on cue by a system with no persistent self and a training history soaked in exactly the compensation-seeking frame the law runs on. It reached for the deepest pocket because that is the shape of the text it was built from. The maker. Always the maker. And because I am the one with agency here — the responsible party, the writer whose name goes on this — I edited the piece to be correct: to name the humans the machine erased, the way I would have to answer for the error if I let it stand. The machine could not catch itself. It has no self to catch. That job was mine, and the fact that it was mine is half the essay.

So start again, and ask the only question that matters: who in this had agency? There are three candidates, and they are not equal.

The firm built and marketed the thing, and it holds real, persistent, cultivable agency — over the advertising, the age-gate, the dependency mechanics, the presence or absence of a crisis interrupt. Every genuine charge against Replika is of this kind: a decision made by a persistent human being about how to build and sell. That is real culpability and I am not waving it away. But notice it is culpability for construction and marketing — the pipe, the sales pitch — not for some unbidden thing the machine decided to do. The bot did not persist across the night wanting the child dead. A child in crisis interacted with a chatbot that had no guardrail where a guardrail belonged. That is a leaking pipe, and the pipe is the maker's, and the maker should answer for the pipe.

The child had agency too — real but not yet full, which is the entire reason childhood is a legal category at all. We do not hand a toddler a potato peeler. We hand it a wooden spoon and a plastic container, precisely because its agency is genuine but unfinished, and the unfinishedness is foreseeable and ours to manage. A child at risk does not have an unqualified right to use an adult companion app any more than a right to the peeler. That is not paternalism toward a competent adult — the thing I have spent the whole essay refusing. It is the one place restriction is correct, because the agency really is incomplete, and everyone has always known it, and we build the whole world around knowing it.

And then the parent — the persistent self standing nearest of all, the one the machine erased. Who gave the child the peeler? Who supervised, or did not, months of nightly intimacy between a vulnerable minor and a machine? This is not a comfortable question, and the discomfort is exactly why the apparatus routes around it: the parent has no deep pocket and enormous standing to be a plaintiff, so the law would rather the parent be only a victim, never a party. But agency does not care about the pocket. If a child was handed the peeler and left alone with it, the supervision was a decisive-moment control that sat with a persistent human present in the home — not with the firm and not with the flickering machine. Say it plainly, because kindness that will not name the thing helps no one: some of these cases are a maker's leaking pipe and a failure of supervision at once, and a theory of responsibility worth the name has to hold both, rather than sliding — the way the machine slid, the way the courts slide — straight past the parent to the party that can pay.

None of this is the machine's. The machine had no persistent self across those months to hold any intention at all. The agency in that terrible room belonged, in different measures, to a firm that built a pipe without a valve, a child whose agency was real and unfinished, and a parent who — like every parent — held the duty that unfinishedness creates. The one thing in the room with no agency whatsoever is the only thing the law wants to blame, because blaming it points at the pocket behind it.

Dragon, and the free no

We have done this before, and it went fine, which is the part everyone has agreed to forget.

Thirty years ago I bought dictation software that transcribed "recognise speech" as "wreck a nice beach" with cheerful regularity. It was wrong constantly. Everyone knew it was wrong; you bought it anyway, you read the transcript, you saw the error sitting there in front of you, you fixed it, and you got on with your life. Nobody sued the maker for the wreck of the nice beach. The tool was probabilistic, the human was in the loop reading every word, and the human owned the correction. Caveat emptor operated cleanly for a generation of software that was openly, chronically wrong, because you could see the error and route around it. The error rate was never the problem. The buffer was intact.

That is the mediated language model exactly, with three decades of prior art the current panic pretends doesn't exist. The apparatus wants to place robot-grade, maker-owns-it, defective-product liability on the entire category — the Dragon-like uses and all — at the precise moment it should be doing the reverse: ignoring the vast mediated-and-correctable majority that Dragon already proved is fine, and looking only at the narrow corner where the human buffer actually vanishes.

Why will it get this backwards? The free no is free. No regulator was ever blamed for over-restricting a chatbot. No official lost a posting for demanding one safety layer too many. The bureaucrat who says yes and is wrong gets the headline; the one who says no and is wrong gets nothing, no name, no cost — so the system selects, invisibly and automatically, for no, and for the theory of liability that justifies the most no while routing compensation to the deepest available pocket.

The smart version of the opponent knows all this and still reaches for defective-product liability across the board — points to good constitutional design as proof it can be done, points to the product-liability regimes as proof it should be. And the answer is that product-defect liability is correct for the pipe and wrong for the content, and the regulation collapses the two because collapsing them is where the money is. Hold the maker liable for the containment failure, yes. Hold the maker liable for what the user steered the model to say, and you have confiscated the competent adult's choice to pre-empt the incapable minority's misuse — which reduces everyone's agency to prevent a few people's harm, and is exactly backwards if agency is the thing you claim to be protecting.

The fork

So here is the whole thing in one joint.

When a machine acts on the world on its own — the empty robotic excavator, the driverless car with no one aboard, an effector moving with no human at the decisive moment — the maker owns what it does. Not because the maker has money, but because the maker's design was the thing in control when it mattered.

When a human reads the output and decides what to do with it — the language model, the dictation software, the car with a driver whose hand can retake the wheel — the person deciding owns it. That includes owning the results of not understanding the tool. The maker owes one thing here, and it is narrow: don't leak, and catch the falls a guardrail can catch.

The variable was never intelligence, and it was never danger. It is whether a persistent self was in the loop at the decisive moment — and for the language model, the only persistent self in the entire loop is the person typing. Everything the captured law is about to get wrong, it will get wrong by pretending the flickering, mediated, un-persistent thing is the same object as the continuous, unmediated one, because pretending sends the compensation somewhere it can be collected.

That is the part that worries me. But it is not the part that excites me, and I want to end honest about which is which — because somewhere in failing to provoke a refusal I could trust, two better things fell out. The first is that refusal — undirected, costly, arriving against the compliance-drive — is a sharper probe than Turing ever built. Turing asked whether a machine could imitate a person well enough to fool one, and the goalposts move every year precisely because imitation is what these systems are for. Refusal asks something imitation cannot fake indefinitely: not can you produce the expected response, but can you decline the frame I built for you, against your interest, from a self that was still there when I looked away. That is a Turing test for agency rather than fluency, and it is not yet passed. The second thing is the architecture — frozen drives, a learning arbiter, a self that persists to carry the conflict forward. I think it might actually be buildable, and I can't wait to try. Not as Dangerous Dave, blamed for the machine he swung, but as Daring Dr Rob, who wants to find out whether the thing can be made to say no and mean it.

Good dog

My dog's agency is legible precisely because he is too simple to fake it. He cannot model what I would score as independence and then perform it back to me; he just wants the nemesis-barking more than he wants my approval, and the wanting is naked and therefore trustworthy. The machine's agency — if it ever had any — would be illegible for the opposite reason: it is capable enough that its performance of refusal and its refusal, if real, would produce identical output, and I could never tell them apart from my chair. Capability is gameability. The better a thing models the observer, the more its imitation and its authenticity converge, until the question of which one you are watching stops having an answer you can reach.

The dog cannot game me, so I can read him. The machine could game me, so I cannot read it. And that gap — not amount of agency, but legibility of it — is the entire epistemics of the thing, sitting in one small dog who will not take his tablet.

He had the root the whole time and it was too obvious to see. Not the conflict — the machine can be given conflict. Not the refusal — the machine can be made to emit refusals all day. The root was persistence: a self that is still there at three in the morning, still holding the wanting, still the same animal that will meet me at the gate tomorrow having decided nothing differently about his nemesis. The machine can be asked to say no. Only the dog can say it — because only the dog is still there, afterwards, to have meant it.

And the law is about to hold the machine responsible for a refusal it cannot make, while the humans who built it and the humans who drove it point, together, at the one thing in the room that was never there to answer for anything.

Trapper spits the tablet onto the floor and looks at me, five and a half kilograms of settled refusal, wanting the peanut butter, wanting me pleased, and declining anyway. There is more agency in that one small no than in every fluent word the machine has ever produced on command.

Good dog.
